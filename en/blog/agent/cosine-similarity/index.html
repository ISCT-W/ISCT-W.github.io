Why are retrieval scores all near 0.9? Cosine similarity and evidence in agents

Separate high-dimensional concentration, shared directions, training temperature, and score transformations to understand what retrieval scores can tell an agent.

WJunhao WeiAbout 6 min read

When an agent searches a knowledge base, the first result might score 0.93, the second 0.92, and the rest roughly 0.9. They all look relevant, yet reading them reveals that some merely share the topic and cannot answer the question.

It is tempting to blame the “curse of dimensionality”: have high-dimensional embeddings made cosine similarity unable to distinguish documents?

Two questions need separating: why scores concentrate, and why they concentrate at a high value. High-dimensional geometry explains concentration under particular assumptions; it does not directly explain 0.9.

Random high-dimensional vectors have cosine similarity near zero

Cosine similarity measures how closely two nonzero vectors point in the same direction:

cos⁡(x,y)=x⊤y∥x∥∥y∥\operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\frac{x^\top y}{\|x\|\|y\|}cos(x,y)=∥x∥∥y∥x⊤y​

For unit vectors, this is their inner product. If x,yx,yx,y are independent and uniform on the unit sphere in ddd dimensions, then:

E[x⊤y]=0,Var⁡(x⊤y)=1d\mathbb E[x^\top y]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(x^\top y)=\frac1dE[x⊤y]=0,Var(x⊤y)=d1​

Rotational symmetry explains this result. Fix one vector's direction: the inner product becomes the other random unit vector's coordinate along that direction. All coordinates are equivalent and their squares sum to one, so each coordinate has second moment 1/d1/d1/d.

In 1024 dimensions, the standard deviation is only about 0.031. As dimension grows, two random directions become nearly perpendicular and their cosine concentrates near zero. Research on random angles on spheres establishes this rigorously. Cai, Fan, and Jiang, 2013

The assumptions matter: independence, uniformity, and a unit sphere. Learned text embeddings generally do not satisfy them. Dimension alone does not determine their similarity distribution.

A shared direction can raise the baseline

Consider a simplified model:

x=α u+1−α ax=\sqrt{\alpha}\,u+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\,ax=α​u+1−α​a y=α u+1−α by=\sqrt{\alpha}\,u+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\,by=α​u+1−α​b

Here u,a,bu,a,bu,a,b are unit vectors, and a,ba,ba,b are perpendicular to uuu. Both vectors contain a shared direction uuu; a,ba,ba,b represent their differing components. Then:

cos⁡(x,y)=α+(1−α)a⊤b\operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\alpha+(1-\alpha)a^\top bcos(x,y)=α+(1−α)a⊤b

If a,ba,ba,b are independent and uniform on the high-dimensional unit sphere perpendicular to uuu, their inner product is near zero. With α=0.9\alpha=0.9α=0.9, the full cosine concentrates near 0.9.

The roles are distinct: the shared direction sets the high baseline; the high-dimensional distribution of the differing components controls its variation. This is a mathematical illustration, not a claim that real models always admit this fixed decomposition.

Early research found substantial directional bias, commonly discussed as anisotropy, in contextual word representations from BERT, ELMo, and GPT-2. That does not imply that all Transformers crowd their representations into one direction: subsequent work provides counterexamples. Ethayarajh, 2019; Anisotropy is Not Inherent to Transformers, 2024

Mean cosine is also an incomplete description of anisotropy. Vectors distributed symmetrically between the two directions of a single line can have mean cosine zero.

Small score gaps can provide a strong training signal

E5's official FAQ describes cosine scores between 0.7 and 1.0 as expected and relates this behavior to the low temperature of 0.01 used in InfoNCE contrastive learning. E5 documentation

A contrastive loss for a positive example can be written as:

L=−log⁡exp⁡(s+/τ)∑jexp⁡(sj/τ)L=-\log\frac{\exp(s_+/\tau)}{\sum_j\exp(s_j/\tau)}L=−log∑j​exp(sj​/τ)exp(s+​/τ)​

Here sjs_jsj​ is the query–candidate similarity and τ\tauτ the temperature. A positive–negative score gap of just 0.05 at temperature 0.01 gives an exponential-term ratio of:

exp⁡(0.050.01)=e5≈148\exp\left(\frac{0.05}{0.01}\right)=e^5\approx148exp(0.010.05​)=e5≈148

A small-looking gap can therefore produce a substantial distinction in the training objective.

However, this calculation only explains how temperature amplifies score differences. It does not prove that low temperature must concentrate cosine scores near 0.9. The data, model, and full training procedure also affect the distribution.

For a fixed query, adding the same constant to every candidate score leaves this softmax unchanged. The objective does not directly assign a relevance probability to 0.9: it creates relative competition between candidates, rather than automatically calibrated confidence.

Two more immediate explanations: score transformations and Top-K

Before analyzing geometry, check what the interface actually displays.

For example, Elasticsearch computes a document's _score as follows when its dense_vector field uses cosine similarity:

_score=1+cos⁡(q,d)2\text{\_score}=\frac{1+\operatorname{cos}(q,d)}2_score=21+cos(q,d)​

In that configuration, a displayed 0.9 corresponds to raw cosine 0.8. The transformation also halves score gaps. Different query types may use different scoring rules, so verify the actual configuration rather than assuming every score field is raw cosine. Elasticsearch documentation

Retrieval also typically displays only the Top-K. Even if scores across the entire corpus span a wide range, the highest few may occupy a narrow interval. Duplicate content or heavily overlapping chunks can make candidates more similar still.

“The ten returned results are near 0.9” and “random unrelated documents are also near 0.9” therefore call for separate checks.

For an agent, the question is whether a document supports the answer

This is an illustration, not a measurement from a real model:

Candidate Cosine score Actual content
A 0.93 Contains the explicit conclusion needed
B 0.92 Discusses the topic but lacks the answer
C 0.91 Applies to a different version and cannot be cited directly

All three scores are high, but their value as evidence differs greatly. Semantic proximity does not guarantee that a document matches the question's version, time, or conditions, or supports the agent's final claim.

When checking a retrieval system, I would proceed in this order:

  1. Confirm the score definition. Manually compute raw cosine for a few vector pairs and compare it with the service output.
  2. Check input processing. Verify compatible query and document models, prefixes, pooling, and truncation of important content. E5, for example, specifies task-dependent prefixes. E5 usage instructions
  3. Compare candidate groups. Inspect random documents, genuinely relevant documents, and hard negatives that share the topic without answering the question—not just the selected Top-K.
  4. Evaluate ranking and abstention separately. Use Recall@K, MRR, or nDCG for retrieval and ranking. To detect insufficient evidence, include unanswerable queries, choose thresholds on a validation set, and confirm performance on a held-out test set.

If the answer is retrieved but ranked poorly, a reranker is worth evaluating. It reads the question and document together for a finer relevance judgment, but its score should not be interpreted as answer-correctness probability without calibration either.

There is no reason to whiten or reduce every vector merely to spread scores out. L2 normalization does not change cosine; monotonically stretching scores does not change their order. Centering, whitening, and dimensionality reduction change geometry and require task evaluation. Research on anisotropy also shows that “more uniform is always better” is not a universal rule. Stable Anisotropic Regularization

When retrieval scores cluster near 0.9, examine their meaning, the separation between relevant and irrelevant documents, and whether candidates actually support an answer. Cosine similarity helps an agent find material worth reading; deciding whether that material is evidence requires another judgment.
