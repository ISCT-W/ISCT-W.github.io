Language models: attention, pretraining, and compute allocation

A language model converts text to tokens and learns to predict the next token from the preceding context.

WJunhao WeiAbout 10 min read

A language model converts text to tokens and learns to predict the next token from the preceding context. Vocabulary, local syntax, and relationships across sentences all pass through the same conditional distribution. Transformers construct contextual representations; pretraining objectives determine how they are learned; model size and data volume determine what can be learned within a compute budget.

Autoregressive modeling and Transformer layers

For a sequence x=(x1,…,xT)x=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)x=(x1​,…,xT​), the probability chain rule gives:

pθ(x)=∏t=1Tpθ(xt∣x<t),Lpretrain=−∑t=1Tlog⁡pθ(xt∣x<t).p_\theta(x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}), \qquad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{pretrain}} =-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}).pθ​(x)=t=1∏T​pθ​(xt​∣x<t​),Lpretrain​=−t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​).

During training, the full sequence is available, so losses at all positions can be computed together. A causal mask prevents looking ahead. During inference, future tokens do not yet exist: append the sampled token and compute the next step. This explains parallel training versus sequential generation.

A typical decoder-only Transformer alternates self-attention and position-wise feed-forward networks. Attention exchanges information across positions, while the FFN transforms each position nonlinearly. Residual connections preserve information and gradient paths, and normalization controls sublayer input scales. The diagram uses a common pre-norm arrangement; actual normalization, activations, and residual placement vary by model. The original Transformer paper defines attention and multi-head attention.

Each residual branch bypasses one sublayer. Token interaction occurs in attention. The FFN shares parameters across positions but does not directly mix them. Inject position information where the architecture specifies: RoPE usually rotates Q/K after projection, whereas sinusoidal absolute encodings are generally added to input representations.

Attention scale and gradients

The unscaled logit of one head is:

s=q⊤k=∑i=1dkqiki.s=q^\top k=\sum_{i=1}^{d_k}q_i k_i.s=q⊤k=i=1∑dk​​qi​ki​.

Under an approximate assumption of independent dimensions with zero mean and unit variance:

E[s]=0,Var⁡(s)=dk,Std⁡(s)=dk.\mathbb{E}[s]=0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(s)=d_k, \qquad \operatorname{Std}(s)=\sqrt{d_k}.E[s]=0,Var(s)=dk​,Std(s)=dk​​.

Consequently:

Var⁡(q⊤kdk)≈1.\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{q^\top k}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\approx 1.Var(dk​​q⊤k​)≈1.

Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ offsets logit growth with head dimension, reducing premature softmax saturation and stabilizing gradients. A standard deviation near one relies on those approximations; it is not a strict guarantee for trained Q/K vectors.

  • No scaling: Standard deviation grows with dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​; softmax may become nearly one-hot and gradients on nonmaximal entries shrink.
  • Divide by dkd_kdk​: Standard deviation becomes about 1/dk1/\sqrt{d_k}1/dk​​, pushing logits toward zero and softmax toward uniformity as dimension grows.
  • Divide by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​: This cancels the standard-deviation growth caused by summation, keeping scales roughly comparable across dimensions.
  • Other constants and learned temperatures are possible. To cancel dimension growth, however, the natural scale is dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​. Variants such as QK-Norm normalize Q/K before controlling temperature.

Adding dkd_kdk​ approximately independent terms adds their variances, giving variance dkd_kdk​ and standard deviation dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​. Attention divides by the standard deviation, not the variance.

Multi-head attention and representation diversity

Multi-head attention projects hidden states into hhh Q/K/V subspaces and computes attention in parallel:

head⁡j=Attention⁡(XWjQ,XWjK,XWjV),\operatorname{head}_j = \operatorname{Attention} \left( XW_j^Q, XW_j^K, XW_j^V \right),headj​=Attention(XWjQ​,XWjK​,XWjV​), MHA⁡(X)=Concat⁡(head⁡1,…,head⁡h)WO.\operatorname{MHA}(X) = \operatorname{Concat} \left( \operatorname{head}_1,\ldots, \operatorname{head}_h \right)W^O.MHA(X)=Concat(head1​,…,headh​)WO.

Heads may learn local or long-range dependencies, syntax, or reference relationships. These are learned patterns, not semantic roles assigned by the architecture.

A common choice is:

dk=dv=dmodelh.d_k=d_v=\frac{d_{\text{model}}}{h}.dk​=dv​=hdmodel​​.

With fixed dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​, adding heads mainly divides the total channels into more subspaces. It does not simply multiply the Q/K/V projection parameters or main attention computation by hhh.

How initialization breaks symmetry between heads

If all head-specific Q/K/V parameters, output-projection paths, and other operations are completely symmetric, the heads produce identical outputs, receive identical gradients, and continue with identical updates:

W1(0)=W2(0),∇W1(t)=∇W2(t)⟹W1(t+1)=W2(t+1).W_1^{(0)}=W_2^{(0)}, \quad \nabla W_1^{(t)}=\nabla W_2^{(t)} \Longrightarrow W_1^{(t+1)}=W_2^{(t+1)}.W1(0)​=W2(0)​,∇W1(t)​=∇W2(t)​⟹W1(t+1)​=W2(t+1)​.

They become repeated copies rather than developing different functions. This is the same symmetry-breaking issue that prevents initializing every neuron with identical weights.

Identical initial Q/K/V alone is not enough to preserve symmetry forever. Different head-specific blocks in WOW^OWO may send different gradients back, breaking symmetry after the first update. The precise statement is:

Identical initialization preserves identical learning only when the entire head-specific computation and gradient paths are symmetric. Independent random initialization deliberately breaks that symmetry.

Initialization and signal propagation

Xavier and Kaiming initialization both aim to prevent activation or gradient variance from exploding or vanishing with depth, but assume different nonlinearities.

Xavier / Glorot

A common normal form is:

Var⁡(W)≈2fanin+fanout.\operatorname{Var}(W) \approx \frac{2}{\mathrm{fan}_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathrm{fan}_{\mathrm{out}}}.Var(W)≈fanin​+fanout​2​.

It balances forward and backward variance and suits linear layers or approximately symmetric activations such as tanh. Transformers combine linear projections, attention, residuals, and LayerNorm/RMSNorm. Xavier can be a starting point for some matrices, but its suitability also depends on activation, depth, and residual scaling.

Kaiming / He

For ReLU, a common normal form is:

Var⁡(W)≈2fanin.\operatorname{Var}(W) \approx \frac{2}{\mathrm{fan}_{\mathrm{in}}}.Var(W)≈fanin​2​.

With approximately symmetric inputs, ReLU zeros roughly half the activations and halves their second moment. Kaiming’s factor of two compensates for this loss. It adjusts overall signal and gradient scales; it cannot restore the individual negative gradients that ReLU truncates. Classical CNNs often pair ReLU with Kaiming initialization.

Match initialization to the actual architecture

  • Xavier: Balances fan_in and fan_out for linear or roughly symmetric activations.
  • Kaiming: Compensates for the variance loss from ReLU truncation.
  • Transformers: Linear projections, residuals, normalization, and GELU or SiLU/SwiGLU often lead to Xavier or model-specific initialization.
  • Classical CNNs: Convolutions followed by ReLU commonly use Kaiming.

These are not hard rules determined by the names “Transformer” and “CNN.” Activations, residual structure, and normalization matter. Modern Transformers may use truncated normal distributions, depth-scaled residual branches, or small output-projection initialization. A ReLU Transformer or GELU CNN should follow its actual architecture rather than a label.

Masks determine where information can flow

The general attention expression is:

Attention⁡(Q,K,V)=softmax⁡(QK⊤dk+M)V.\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) =\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\right)V.Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​+M)V.

A causal mask for four tokens is:

M=[0−∞−∞−∞00−∞−∞000−∞0000].M=\begin{bmatrix} 0&-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\\ 0&0&-\infty&-\infty\\ 0&0&0&-\infty\\ 0&0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}.M=​0000​−∞000​−∞−∞00​−∞−∞−∞0​​.

Row iii can access only tokens at or before iii; softmax gives masked positions zero weight. Padding masks instead exclude filler positions from useful aggregation. These are separate purposes.

A bidirectional encoder uses both left and right context and suits representation learning, classification, and extraction. BERT masks part of its input and predicts the hidden content. Predicting a token from an unmasked sentence that already contains it does not demonstrate generation ability. Encoder–decoder systems combine bidirectional input understanding with causal output generation, so bidirectional representations and generation are compatible. See the BERT paper.

A prefix LM permits bidirectional attention within the prefix, while generated tokens see the full prefix and earlier outputs. Sequence-to-sequence models read encoder states through cross-attention. Both must prevent access to future target information. Decoder-only models unify tasks as text continuation, simplifying the training and usage interface; this does not make other architectures inferior on every task.

Attention cost and the KV cache

Full self-attention forms pairwise relations among LLL positions, with core matrix work about O(L2d)O(L^2d)O(L2d). Storing every attention weight directly takes O(L2)O(L^2)O(L2) space. Efficient kernels reduce intermediate storage and data movement, but exact full attention still computes all positional interactions. Memory savings do not imply linear computation.

During autoregressive generation, historical K/V projections can be cached. A new query matches historical keys and aggregates historical values. This saves repeated computation but consumes more memory as context length, layer count, and KV-head count grow. MHA has K/V heads corresponding to each query head; MQA shares one K/V group, while GQA shares by groups. They primarily reduce KV storage and memory traffic; actual speed depends on batching, length, and implementation. See the GQA paper.

Pretraining data, loss, and capability limits

Pretraining mainly maximizes next-token likelihood over large text collections. Lower loss means better token prediction on the evaluated corpus. The objective does not directly encode factual truth, user intent, or reliable citations. A model may learn useful regularities alongside outdated facts, duplication, and source biases; task-specific evaluations distinguish these effects.

A masked negative log-likelihood can be written as:

LNLL=−∑tmtlog⁡πθ(yt∣x,y<t).\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NLL}}=-\sum_t m_t\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).LNLL​=−t∑​mt​logπθ​(yt​∣x,y<t​).

During pretraining, mtm_tmt​ commonly masks padding or invalid positions. During SFT, it may additionally mask user input so that only assistant responses receive supervision. The mathematical forms are similar; the differences lie in data distributions, conditioning, and supervised positions. SFT usually follows pretraining to shape behavior with relatively few high-quality demonstrations using already learned language representations.

Data processing changes the distribution actually seen by the model. Duplicate documents overweight samples, evaluation contamination exaggerates generalization, and language/domain proportions shift capabilities. Track pretraining loss, downstream performance, and data quality together: a single average loss cannot show that every domain is improving.

Scaling laws under a finite budget

Chinchilla studies the allocation of parameter count NNN and training tokens DDD under fixed training compute CCC. A rough relationship for dense Transformers is:

C∝ND.C\propto ND.C∝ND.

An oversized, undertrained model spends compute on unused capacity; a small model trained for too long may be capacity-limited. An empirical scaling law can describe loss as:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDγ,A,B,α,γ>0.L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\gamma}}, \qquad A,B,\alpha,\gamma>0.L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DγB​,A,B,α,γ>0.

EEE is the fitted irreducible term; the remaining terms describe errors from insufficient capacity and data. Optimizing under the compute constraint suggests candidate model sizes and training lengths, but coefficients and exponents must be fitted to the target data and model family. In its setting, Chinchilla found roughly proportional growth of parameters and data, with about 20 training tokens per parameter.

That ratio is not a universal upper limit. For a model used many times after deployment, total cost also includes inference. A smaller model trained more extensively may reduce lifetime cost. Data quality, objectives, context length, and sparsity shift the optimum; MoE total and per-token active parameters must not be conflated.

For example, doubling parameters while halving tokens leaves the rough NDNDND budget unchanged but need not give the same loss. The two error terms determine which allocation is preferable. Scaling laws guide experiments; small-scale runs, held-out evaluation, and cost measurements must establish applicability.
