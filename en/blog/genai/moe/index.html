Mixture of Experts: expand capacity with sparse routing

Mixture of Experts replaces some shared computation with input-dependent expert computation.

WJunhao WeiAbout 5 min read

Mixture of Experts replaces some shared computation with input-dependent expert computation. In language models, several feed-forward networks commonly replace a single FFN in selected Transformer layers, and a router selects a few experts per token. This increases stored parameter capacity while activating only part of it for each token.

An expert is an independently parameterized subnetwork. It does not inherently correspond to a human domain such as medicine, mathematics, or programming. Specialization emerges from routing and updates and may follow lexical, syntactic, representational, or other statistical patterns.

From a dense FFN to sparse computation

For token representation xxx and EEE experts EiE_iEi​, the router emits logits g(x)g(x)g(x), computes softmax probabilities, and selects the top kkk:

S(x)=TopK⁡(g(x)),y=∑i∈S(x)pi(x)Ei(x).S(x)=\operatorname{TopK}(g(x)), \qquad y=\sum_{i\in S(x)}p_i(x)E_i(x).S(x)=TopK(g(x)),y=i∈S(x)∑​pi​(x)Ei​(x).

Implementations differ in pip_ipi​: some retain the original softmax probabilities, while others renormalize over selected experts. This changes output scales and router gradients and must be reproduced consistently.

Routing decides where to send tokens; expert execution performs their computation. Different tokens can select different combinations. Combining results must restore original token order and sum the weighted outputs for each token. Attention, normalization, and output layers usually remain shared.

For probabilities (0.60,0.25,0.10,0.05)(0.60,0.25,0.10,0.05)(0.60,0.25,0.10,0.05), top-2 chooses the first two experts. Renormalizing within that selection gives about (0.706,0.294)(0.706,0.294)(0.706,0.294). The other experts do not run an FFN for this token. These probabilities describe one routing event, not permanent semantic expert roles.

Costs of top-1 and top-2

Top-1 sends each token to one expert, reducing computation and communication and simplifying capacity planning. Switch Transformers demonstrates this simplified routing at large scale.

Top-2 combines two experts, offering richer computation paths at the usual cost of more expert FLOPs, dispatch traffic, and combination work. It does not automatically balance load: many tokens may still choose the same pair. Choose top-kkk with quality, routing stability, and hardware throughput in mind.

Selecting the top-kkk set is discrete. Common methods differentiate continuous weights for selected experts and train the router with a balancing objective. Do not assume every expert receives the same full gradient as in an ordinary all-expert softmax mixture.

Total parameters, active parameters, and memory

Let the shared part have PsP_sPs​ parameters and each of EEE experts have PeP_ePe​, with kkk active experts per token. Ignoring routers and layer differences:

Ptotal≈Ps+EPe,Pactive/token≈Ps+kPe.P_{\mathrm{total}}\approx P_s+EP_e, \qquad P_{\mathrm{active/token}}\approx P_s+kP_e.Ptotal​≈Ps​+EPe​,Pactive/token​≈Ps​+kPe​.

For Ps=2P_s=2Ps​=2, Pe=1P_e=1Pe​=1, E=8E=8E=8, and k=2k=2k=2, in billions of parameters, the model stores about 10 billion and activates about 4 billion per token. This extra capacity does not mean that only 4 billion parameters’ weights need storage.

All expert weights must reside on some devices or storage tier. Training additionally needs gradients and optimizer states; inference needs KV cache, activations, routing buffers, and batch memory. Distributing experts spreads storage but adds communication. Loading on demand may be constrained by bandwidth and latency.

Different tokens in one batch may collectively visit every expert. Few active parameters per token therefore does not mean few experts read per batch. Compare dense and MoE models using total and active parameters, FLOPs, memory, and measured latency together.

Why imbalance can erase the gain

An early router preference sends more tokens and gradients to a few experts, leaving others undertrained and reinforcing imbalance. Even if the average load is small, the busiest expert can delay the whole layer, because output combination generally waits for dispatched work.

Switch’s top-1 auxiliary balancing term can be written as:

Lbalance=αE∑i=1EfiPi,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{balance}} =\alpha E\sum_{i=1}^{E}f_iP_i,Lbalance​=αEi=1∑E​fi​Pi​,

For a routing batch of TTT tokens:

fi=1T∑x1{argmax⁡g(x)=i},Pi=1T∑xpi(x).f_i=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{x}\mathbf1\{\operatorname{argmax}g(x)=i\}, \qquad P_i=\frac{1}{T}\sum_x p_i(x).fi​=T1​x∑​1{argmaxg(x)=i},Pi​=T1​x∑​pi​(x).

fif_ifi​ is the fraction actually assigned to expert iii, while PiP_iPi​ is its mean routing probability. The auxiliary term discourages concentrating further probability on already overloaded experts; the task loss learns useful representations. This balances quality and load, not uniform expert use by every token. Too much weight may overpower the task objective; too little may leave hotspots.

Capacity, overflow, and communication

Some implementations assign fixed expert capacities for easier parallel execution. A common top-1 approximation is:

capacity≈⌈capacity factor⋅TE⌉.\mathrm{capacity}\approx \left\lceil\mathrm{capacity\ factor}\cdot\frac{T}{E}\right\rceil.capacity≈⌈capacity factor⋅ET​⌉.

A larger capacity factor reduces overflow but increases buffering and potentially empty-slot computation. Top-kkk capacity must also account for total dispatches, with details set by the implementation. Overflow policies vary: skip the expert transformation while retaining the residual, reroute, or use dynamic/no-drop capacity. No single policy is inherent to MoE.

Expert parallelism groups tokens by expert and often uses all-to-all communication to send them to the owning devices and back. Small batches can leave each expert with too few tokens for efficient matrix multiplication. Larger batches amortize costs but may increase waiting and memory. Online costs depend jointly on topology, bandwidth, routing distribution, and latency goals.

How to evaluate sparsity

Compare matched budgets. At fixed training FLOPs, ask whether additional total parameters improve quality. At fixed quality, compare training cost. At fixed hardware and workload, compare throughput, first-token latency, and tail latency. Parameter count alone is not an evaluation.

Track expert token shares, overflow, and gradients during training. During inference, measure communication and hotspots across prompts and lengths. One successful balancing-loss experiment does not establish universal effectiveness. Change only that factor under the same data and budget to identify its benefit and cost more clearly.
