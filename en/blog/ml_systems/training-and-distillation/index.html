Training stability and distillation: diagnose the failure before choosing a fix

Numerical instability and unsuccessful capability transfer may both appear as degraded scores, but require different diagnoses.

WJunhao WeiAbout 4 min read

Numerical instability and unsuccessful capability transfer may both appear as degraded scores, but require different diagnoses. The first calls for inspecting batches, activations, gradients, and optimizer state. The second calls for examining teacher signals, student capacity, and data coverage. This article treats stability and distillation separately, then connects them through layered evaluation.

For numerical anomalies, find the first computation or state that becomes invalid. For distillation, identify what the student learned and which capabilities were lost. Neither training loss nor an average score alone is sufficient.

Locate instability layer by layer

Instability can arise from residual-path scales, initialization, normalization placement, learning rate, mixed-precision overflow, unusual data, or distributed communication. First locate the layer and step where NaN/Inf or a loss spike appears, then inspect normalization, initialization, the optimizer, precision, and data.

Key mechanisms

  1. Pre-LN versus Post-LN: Pre-LN normalizes before attention/FFN, leaving a residual backbone closer to identity and often easing deep gradient propagation. Post-LN has different representational properties but is more sensitive to initialization and warmup.
  2. Normalization type and location are separate: RMSNorm uses root-mean-square scaling as an alternative to LayerNorm. Pre-LN/Post-LN describes placement before or after a sublayer. RMSNorm is not a synonym for Pre-LN.
  3. Initialization and residual scaling: Large weights can accumulate activation variance with depth. Depth-dependent initialization, residual-branch scaling, DeepNorm, or related methods may help.
  4. Learning rate and optimizer: Warmup lets optimizer states and representations develop smoothly. Peak learning rate, Adam’s β/ε, weight decay, and gradient clipping also matter.
  5. Precision: FP16 has a narrow exponent range and can overflow. BF16 is often more robust but has fewer mantissa bits. Monitor loss scale, gradient norms, activation maxima, and nonfinite values.
  6. Data and distribution: Extreme lengths or token distributions, incorrect labels, duplication/contamination, and all-reduce or checkpoint-restoration errors can all look like loss spikes.

Diagnosis order

From teacher signals to a student model

Distillation trains a student on a teacher’s output distribution, intermediate representations, or generated sequences rather than hard labels alone. Soft targets contain relative relationships between classes or tokens and can transfer capabilities into a smaller model.

Common forms

  1. Logit distillation
LKD=T2 DKL(softmax⁡(zt/T)∥softmax⁡(zs/T))\mathcal L_{KD}=T^2\,D_{KL} \left( \operatorname{softmax}(z_t/T) \Vert \operatorname{softmax}(z_s/T) \right)LKD​=T2DKL​(softmax(zt​/T)∥softmax(zs​/T))

A higher temperature TTT smooths the distribution, exposing relationships among nonmaximal classes.

  1. Feature distillation: Align attention maps, hidden states, or selected intermediate representations.
  2. Sequence-level distillation: Train with SFT on teacher-generated responses or reasoning traces; synthetic-data distillation is common for LLMs.
  3. Preference/behavior distillation: Learn a teacher’s style, refusals, safety behavior, or tool choices.

Risks and evaluation

  • Teacher biases and hallucinations can enter the training set.
  • Limited student capacity may lose long-tail capabilities first.
  • Insufficient synthetic-data diversity can cause mode collapse.
  • Average benchmarks may hide losses in safety, calibration, or robustness.
  • Closed-model teachers also require attention to output terms and data permissions.
