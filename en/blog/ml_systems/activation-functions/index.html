Activation Functions: Understanding Gradients Through Function Shape

Activation functions turn compositions of linear maps into nonlinear models and change the scale of gradients passing through layers.

WJunhao WeiAbout 7 min read

Activation functions turn compositions of linear maps into nonlinear models and change the scale of gradients passing through layers. Values and derivatives reveal saturation, negative-side truncation, and smooth gating. Replacing an activation also requires considering initialization, residual paths, and pretrained feature distributions.

  • Activations introduce nonlinearity and determine local gradient behavior.
  • Output transforms map logits to probabilities, positive values, or other constrained quantities, as with Sigmoid, Softmax, or Softplus.
  • Loss functions compare predictions with supervision and define the optimization objective.

The same function can play different roles. Sigmoid can be a hidden activation or a binary probability transform. Stable binary training usually uses BCEWithLogitsLoss rather than explicitly applying Sigmoid before BCE.

Function values and derivatives

The plot shows saturation at both ends for Sigmoid/Tanh, zero gradients on ReLU's negative half-line, and a region of nonzero negative-side gradients for GELU/SiLU.

Sigmoid

σ(x)=11+e−x,σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x)).\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}, \qquad \sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)\left(1-\sigma(x)\right).σ(x)=1+e−x1​,σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x)).

Its derivative satisfies 0<σ′(x)≤1/40<\sigma'(x)\leq1/40<σ′(x)≤1/4 and approaches zero for large ∣x∣|x|∣x∣. Repeated multiplication across layers can make gradients vanish.

Uses

  • Binary or multilabel probability outputs.
  • Gates in LSTMs, GRUs, and GLUs.
  • Quantities constrained to (0,1)(0,1)(0,1).

Cautions

  • Both tails saturate.
  • Non-zero-centered outputs can impede hidden-layer optimization.
  • Direct exponentiation or log(sigmoid(x)) risks overflow/underflow.
  • Prefer stable logits-based binary losses.

Tanh is zero-centered with range (−1,1)(-1,1)(−1,1), but still saturates at large positive and negative inputs. It suits recurrent states and signed bounded outputs; zero-centering does not eliminate vanishing gradients.

ReLU

ReLU⁡(x)=max⁡(0,x),ReLU⁡′(x)={0,x<0,1,x>0.\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x), \qquad \operatorname{ReLU}'(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\ 1,&x>0. \end{cases}ReLU(x)=max(0,x),ReLU′(x)={0,1,​x<0,x>0.​

At zero, implementations choose a subgradient; this nondifferentiability is usually not the main practical problem.

Advantages

  • Unsaturated positive side with derivative 1.
  • Cheap computation.
  • Zero negative outputs produce sparse activations.
  • A classic CNN/MLP baseline with He/Kaiming initialization.

Dying ReLU

If a neuron stays negative for all inputs, its gradient remains zero and it may stop updating. Causes include excessive learning rates, very negative biases, unsuitable initialization, and input-distribution shifts.

Mitigations include lowering the learning rate, checking initialization, trying Leaky ReLU/PReLU/ELU/GELU/SiLU, using appropriate normalization and residual paths, and monitoring each layer's zero-activation fraction rather than loss alone.

The positive side is unbounded, so large activations or gradients remain possible. Avoiding positive saturation does not prevent gradient explosion.

Leaky ReLU uses a fixed small α>0\alpha>0α>0; PReLU learns it. Negative-side gradients mitigate dying units, but PReLU adds parameters and may need regularization or constraints. Neither fixes learning-rate, initialization, or overflow issues automatically.

ELU, SELU, and Softplus

ELU is:

ELU⁡(x)={x,x>0,α(ex−1),x≤0.\operatorname{ELU}(x)= \begin{cases} x,&x>0,\\ \alpha(e^x-1),&x\leq 0. \end{cases}ELU(x)={x,α(ex−1),​x>0,x≤0.​

Its negative side saturates at −α-\alpha−α, permitting negative outputs and generally smoother behavior than ReLU. Exponentials cost more, and gradients can still be tiny in the negative tail.

SELU's self-normalizing behavior depends on conditions including initialization, architecture, and AlphaDropout. Swapping it into an ordinary ReLU network does not automatically stabilize training.

Softplus is a smooth ReLU approximation:

Softplus⁡(x)=log⁡(1+ex),Softplus⁡′(x)=σ(x).\operatorname{Softplus}(x)=\log(1+e^x), \qquad \operatorname{Softplus}'(x)=\sigma(x).Softplus(x)=log(1+ex),Softplus′(x)=σ(x).

It is differentiable everywhere and positive, useful for standard deviations or rates. It creates no exact zeros and still has small negative-tail gradients. Use a stable softplus implementation rather than exponentiating large inputs directly.

GELU is common in Transformers, and SiLU in modern LLMs, vision, and diffusion models. Follow pretrained architecture and validation rather than assuming an absolute winner.

Three limits on gradient propagation

Sigmoid's derivative is at most 1/41/41/4 and approaches zero in both tails, so repeated products attenuate early-layer gradients. Gates still use it because they need values in (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), with residual or state paths supporting information flow.

ReLU has derivative one on the positive side but zero on the negative side. Persistently negative neurons can die. It removes positive saturation, not every gradient problem.

GELU and SiLU smoothly suppress negative inputs while retaining some negative values and gradients. Their empirical success depends on the full architecture and training setup, not the activation in isolation.

Match initialization to activation

  • ReLU families commonly use He/Kaiming initialization.
  • Tanh/Sigmoid often use Xavier/Glorot, though deep saturation remains possible.
  • Transformers also require attention to residual branches, norm placement, depth scaling, and warmup.
  • Replacing activations changes variances and pretrained feature distributions; changing one setting is not functional equivalence.
