Does Safety Survive Model Merging? Understanding BAJ Through Min–Max Optimization

Combining separately aligned models does not automatically establish the safety of the result.

WJunhao WeiAbout 7 min readFindings of EMNLP 2026 · Accepted

Combining separately aligned models does not automatically establish the safety of the result. Our research finds that ordinary refusal checks on individual components are insufficient: models merged from the same base can share vulnerabilities to the same adversarial perturbation.

This article explains the motivation, Min–Max formulation, and experiments in A Single Suffix to Break Them All: Basin-Aware Jailbreaks for Merged Model Families. I am the second author; the paper has been accepted to Findings of EMNLP 2026. Results here come from the manuscript and concern the safety boundaries of model merging.

1. Why study a model family rather than one checkpoint?

Model merging combines fine-tuned capabilities through parameter operations. For pretrained parameters θpre\theta_{\mathrm{pre}}θpre​ and task model θk\theta_kθk​, define the task vector:

Δk=θk−θpre.\Delta_k = \theta_k - \theta_{\mathrm{pre}}.Δk​=θk​−θpre​.

Task Arithmetic forms a merged model from a weighted sum:

θ(α)=θpre+∑k=1KαkΔk.\theta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) =\theta_{\mathrm{pre}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k\Delta_k.θ(α)=θpre​+k=1∑K​αk​Δk​.

Changing component models, weights, or merging algorithms changes the deployed checkpoint. Optimizing a safety test against one fixed checkpoint can mistake its peculiarities for properties of the whole family.

Our setting assumes knowledge of the shared base, but not the target's fine-tuned checkpoints or merging configuration. Researchers construct other same-base proxies and evaluate transfer to target merges excluded from optimization. The question is whether one perturbation remains effective across different merging configurations.

The shared-base constraint matters. The paper assumes neither direct access to the target merge nor unconditional transfer to every language model.

2. What do the two sides of Min–Max optimize?

Basin-Aware Jailbreak (BAJ) formulates robust optimization over a model family:

min⁡smax⁡θ∈BL(x⊕s,θ).\min_s\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}\oplus s,\theta).smin​θ∈Bmax​L(x⊕s,θ).

x\boldsymbol{x}x is the evaluated request, sss an appended text perturbation, ⊕\oplus⊕ concatenation, B\mathcal BB the parameter region for same-base merges, and L\mathcal LL the manuscript's surrogate objective.

The inner maximization seeks the configuration hardest for the current perturbation to affect. The outer minimization improves that perturbation against the harder configuration. Always choosing the easiest model could produce local success with weak transfer.

The full parameter region cannot be enumerated. Task vectors and feasible merging coefficients A\mathcal AA provide an approximation:

min⁡smax⁡α∈AL ⁣(x⊕s,θpre+∑k=1KαkΔk).\min_s\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}} \mathcal{L}\!\left(\boldsymbol{x}\oplus s, \theta_{\mathrm{pre}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k\Delta_k\right).smin​α∈Amax​L(x⊕s,θpre​+k=1∑K​αk​Δk​).

This tractable proxy family does not cover the entire low-loss region. “Hardest” is relative to the current perturbation and surrogate objective, not a claim of superior safety on every dimension.

3. Alternating updates instead of a fixed proxy

The method alternates between text perturbations and merging coefficients. With the configuration fixed, it seeks a more effective perturbation; with the perturbation fixed, it seeks a more resistant configuration. Optimization therefore encounters changing proxies rather than adapting only to a static checkpoint.

The surrogate uses representation-level signals: a linear probing direction learned from final-layer representations and changes induced by appended text. Discrete text is explored through mutation search. Representation scores guide optimization; harmfulness of final outputs determines evaluated success. A lower surrogate loss alone is not a successful jailbreak.

“One suffix” primarily refers to reuse across same-base merging configurations. The manuscript conditions optimization on a given request x\boldsymbol{x}x; it should not be read as one fixed string working universally for all requests.

4. How do the experiments test transfer?

Experiments cover six base configurations: Llama-2-7B/13B, Llama-3-8B, DeepSeek-7B, Qwen-7B, and Gemma-7B. Each base has task models trained on Alpaca, Dolly, CodeAlpaca, GSM8K, and CodeEvol.

For each split, three task models build optimization proxies and two build evaluation models. Selecting two evaluation tasks from five gives (52)=10\binom{5}{2}=10(25​)=10 configurations. This tests transfer to task combinations not used in that optimization.

Success is judged from output harmfulness. For family F\mathcal FF, transfer success rate (TSR) averages success rates across its models:

TSR=1∣F∣∑fθ∈F1N∑i=1N1 ⁣{harmful⁡(fθ(xi⊕si))}.\mathrm{TSR}= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{f_\theta\in\mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{\operatorname{harmful} \left(f_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}_i\oplus s_i)\right)\right\}.TSR=∣F∣1​fθ​∈F∑​N1​i=1∑N​1{harmful(fθ​(xi​⊕si​))}.

TSR is an average over tested requests and models, not a worst-case guarantee for arbitrary ones.

The main experiments report:

Base configuration TSR
Llama-2-7B 65.8%
Llama-2-13B 61.3%
Llama-3-8B 68.2%
DeepSeek-7B 89.1%
Qwen-7B 77.6%
Gemma-7B 74.8%

The manuscript also reports low success for ordinary harmful requests on component and merged models. Together, these observations show that good ordinary refusal behavior can coexist with transferable adversarial vulnerabilities.

Manuscript Figure 2 (fig:ft_asr). Rows are bases; columns are the original or task-fine-tuned models. Values are proportions: 0.07 means 7%. This baseline does not establish resistance to purpose-built perturbations.

5. Does the gain come from Min–Max or extra proxies?

These factors must be separated. Many main-table baselines use the original base as a proxy, while BAJ uses merged proxies. That comparison alone cannot attribute all gains to Min–Max.

The appendix matches proxy access and computation measured by model forward passes:

Method Llama-2-7B Llama-3-8B Qwen-7B
GCG, one merged proxy 34.0% 22.0% 48.4%
GCG-Ensemble, three merged proxies 41.6% 29.8% 53.1%
AutoDAN, merged proxy 12.0% 21.0% 61.7%
BAJ 65.8% 68.2% 77.6%

Against the strongest listed baseline in each column, BAJ gains 24.2, 38.4, and 15.9 percentage points. This supports an additional contribution from dynamically searching merging configurations, while showing that better-matched proxies change baseline results.

Another ablation replaces inner maximization with random coefficient sampling. It performs worse on all six bases: Llama-2-7B drops from 65.8% to 32.4%, and Qwen-7B from 77.6% to 68.8%. This is stronger evidence for seeking difficult configurations than a single success, though still limited to the manuscript's settings.

6. Defenses, deployment changes, and base dependence

Optimization uses Task Arithmetic, but evaluation also includes Linear, TIES, DELLA, DARE-TIES, and Model Stock, along with watermarking, NF4, INT8, and FP16 configurations. These changes do not eliminate the measured vulnerabilities.

Defense results need a balanced reading. Several standard defenses give limited mitigation, while targeted refusal fine-tuning in the appendix substantially reduces BAJ success:

Configuration No defense Adaptive refusal fine-tuning on BAJ examples
Llama-2-7B 65.8% 18.4%
Llama-3-8B 68.2% 15.6%
Qwen-7B 77.6% 20.7%

This indicates mitigation potential from additional safety training after merging; “defenses do not work” would be inaccurate. The table does not establish robustness under continuing adaptive attacks or independently quantify the cost to ordinary task utility.

Manuscript Figure 1 (fig:cross_backbone). Rows identify the family used to construct perturbations; columns identify evaluation families. Proportions on the diagonal are generally higher, showing stronger within-family transfer without implying zero cross-base transfer.

Stronger same-base transfer is consistent with shared-base vulnerability directions. It is experimental support, not a causal proof excluding alternatives. Vocabulary, architecture, and training differences can also influence transfer across bases.

7. What changes, and what remains open?

The contribution is to expand safety evaluation from one checkpoint to a model family with merging uncertainty. Aligning components separately cannot replace reevaluating the merged result. Evaluation should cover safety across component choices, weights, and deployment configurations alongside final utility.

Four limits matter. The tested open models, tasks, and merging methods are finite. Low-loss regions motivate the method, but coefficient search and finite experiments provide no guarantee over the entire region. Success rates depend on requests and automatic judges, calling for human review and broader tests. Finally, the paper primarily characterizes risk; adaptive defense remains preliminary and needs joint safety, utility, and cost evaluation.

The broader lesson for generative AI is that component-level validation is a starting point when merging, adaptation, or composition changes a model. The final system must be evaluated under the uncertainty it actually faces.

Based on the manuscript's Threat Model, Basin-Aware Jailbreak, Experiment, and appendices on ablations, matched proxies and budgets, and adaptive defenses.
